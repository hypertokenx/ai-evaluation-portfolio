# Clear Explanation Example: Confirmation Bias

Confirmation bias is the tendency to favor information that supports what we already believe while giving less attention, less weight, or less credibility to information that challenges those beliefs.

It can affect three stages of how we process information:

* **Seeking information** — We may look for sources or examples that support our existing view.
* **Interpreting information** — We may interpret ambiguous evidence in a way that favors what we already believe.
* **Remembering information** — We may more easily remember information that confirms our expectations and forget or discount information that contradicts them.

**Simple example**

Imagine someone believes that a particular diet is the best way to lose weight. They may pay close attention to success stories from people who used that diet while dismissing studies or personal experiences that produce mixed or negative results.

The problem is not that the person necessarily ignores all opposing evidence. Instead, they may unconsciously give supporting evidence more attention or consider it more convincing.

**Why it matters**

Confirmation bias can lead to poor decisions because it makes it easier to build confidence in an existing belief without adequately testing it. It can affect everyday decisions, workplace judgments, financial choices, scientific reasoning, and how people evaluate information online.

**How to reduce it**

One useful approach is to deliberately look for evidence that could prove your belief wrong. Instead of asking only, "What supports my conclusion?", ask:

> "What evidence would make me change my mind?"

This does not eliminate confirmation bias, but it can make the evaluation of competing evidence more deliberate and balanced.
